## Bottom line

Student A's next instructional problem is not learning more isolated letter names or sounds. She reportedly knows uppercase and lowercase letters in both directions—symbol to sound and sound to symbol—and her Khan history strongly supports that conclusion. Her next bridge is to coordinate those known correspondences **in order across an entire printed consonant-vowel-consonant (CVC) word**.

The single best next lesson is:

1. **Blend Sounds 2 — Basic** (Preschool Age 4 or Kindergarten placement)

It is the smallest productive step because it directly targets ordered three-phoneme blending, and Student A has already moved from 85% to 92%. Under the family's mastery policy, one more score of at least 90% establishes mastery and unlocks **Blend Sounds 2 — Main**. It is both instructionally central and very likely within her zone of proximal development.

The best **new** bridge after that is:

2. **Short Vowel Sound a — Basic**
3. **Words with a — Main**
4. **Words with m & n — Main**

These move from the middle vowel in isolation to its position in printed CVC words, then add final-position analysis with consonants that can be held continuously. This directly addresses the pattern in which Student A did well on initial letters and three-sound blending, but scored 50% on **Middle Sound — Basic** and 58% on **Ending Sound — Basic**.

Do not jump yet to **Isolate All Sounds**, **1-Syllable Words**, or consonant blends. Those are sensible later destinations, but they currently introduce too many demands at once.

## What the evidence says should come after letter-sound mastery

The strongest sources converge on the same transition:

- Teach phoneme awareness explicitly, but connect it to letters. The National Reading Panel found phonemic-awareness instruction improved reading and spelling, and was more effective when letters were present and when instruction concentrated on one or two manipulation types rather than many at once. Its meta-analysis covered 52 studies and reported effects of 0.86 on phonemic awareness, 0.53 on reading, and 0.59 on spelling. ([Ehri et al., 2001](https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1598/RRQ.36.3.2); [NICHD summary](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/publications/pubs/nrp/findings))
- Link segmentation and blending to print as soon as the child knows a few letter sounds. The IES/What Works Clearinghouse guide explicitly recommends word building to connect letter-sound knowledge with phonemic awareness, beginning with CVC words before CCVC, CVCC, or silent-e forms. ([IES/WWC foundational-skills guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/practiceGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf))
- Blend from left to right through the word. The same guide recommends simple familiar CVC words first, modeling and feedback, then gradual movement to longer and unfamiliar words. It describes smooth sounding-out—holding and connecting sounds—as preferable to producing isolated sounds separated by pauses. ([IES/WWC guide, Recommendation 3](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/practiceGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf))
- Keep early difficulty controlled. Continuous consonants such as /m/, /n/, /f/, /s/, and /l/ can be held without distortion and are easier to connect to a following vowel than stop consonants. A controlled study also found connected phonation more effective than segmented phonation for teaching beginning readers to decode unfamiliar words. ([Gonzalez-Frey & Ehri, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/10888438.2020.1776290))
- Move toward printed word recognition, not endless oral exercises. Orthographic mapping—the process by which a written word becomes automatically retrievable—depends on connecting graphemes in a spelling to phonemes in its pronunciation. Grapheme-phoneme knowledge plus decoding and phonemic segmentation support that process. ([Ehri, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/rrq.334))
- Use a cumulative sequence. As a concrete example of a modern explicit phonics scope, UFLI begins with a small set of letters and CVC words, then adds letters and CCVC/CVCC forms before more complex spelling patterns. This is an illustrative implementation, not evidence that its exact letter order is uniquely correct. ([UFLI Foundations](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/foundations/); [scope and sequence](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/UFLI-Scope-Sequence.pdf))

This makes the instructional target more precise than “ordering.” Student A needs to stabilize three related operations:

1. **Blending:** `/m/ /a/ /p/` → *map* while preserving left-to-right order.
2. **Segmentation/position:** *map* → first /m/, middle /a/, last /p/.
3. **Phoneme-grapheme integration:** recognizing that changing one grapheme in one position changes the spoken word.

## Recommended Khan-only path

### Phase 1: finish the closest open loops

Keep the working set small. These four lessons are the immediate queue, in this priority order:

| Priority | Khan lesson | Why now | Gate |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Blend Sounds 2 — Basic** | Directly practices the ordered three-phoneme blend; prior scores 85%, 92%. | Another ≥90%: remove Basic and assign Main. |
| 2 | **Make New Words — Basic** | Changes one phoneme while preserving the rest of the sequence; prior score 92%. | Another ≥90%: remove Basic and assign Main. |
| 3 | **Words: End Sound — Main** | Transfers a mastered Basic skill to the next rung; current 83%. | Repeat Main; do not advance until its mastery gate is met. |
| 4 | **Blend Syllables — Main** | Current 91%; a low-risk confidence item that can soon be cleared from the queue. | Another ≥90%: remove Main and assign Practice 1, but keep it secondary to CVC work. |

Do not add all four families' next rungs simultaneously. A promotion replaces the mastered rung; it should not make the queue larger.

### Phase 2: bridge the weak middle and final positions to print

After Phase 1 produces its next data—or immediately if one new diagnostic slot is desired—use this sequence:

| Order | Khan lesson | Function | Why the inferential gap is small |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Short Vowel Sound a — Basic** | Confirm the short-vowel sound that will occupy the middle position. | One known grapheme and one sound; no three-position analysis yet. |
| 2 | **Words with a — Main** | Identify/use /a/ in the middle of printed CVC words. | Adds only word position around the just-checked vowel. This family has no Basic variant. |
| 3 | **Short Vowel Sound i — Basic** | Add a second short vowel. | Preserves the same task and changes one grapheme-sound target. |
| 4 | **Words with i — Main** | Transfer short /i/ into the middle of CVC words. | Same printed CVC structure as the preceding *a* lesson. |
| 5 | **Words with m & n — Main** | Attend to final graphemes in CVC words. | /m/ and /n/ are continuous sounds, so their boundaries are comparatively easy to hear and blend. This family has no Basic variant. |
| 6 | **Words with p & s — Main** | Generalize final-position analysis to new consonants. | Retains one continuous sound (/s/) while introducing a stop sound (/p/). |

Use only one new lesson at a time alongside the Phase 1 reassessments. The `a → Words with a → i → Words with i` order is a cautious local sequence, not a claim that all children must learn vowels in that universal order.

If a Short Vowel Basic lesson is immediately mastered, proceed to its **Main**, then **Practice 1** under the existing Basic → Main → Practice 1 policy. For the `Words with …` CVC groups, which have no Basic variant, Main is necessarily the entry point.

### Phase 3: return to whole-sequence analysis

Once Student A earns at least strong scores on two CVC-middle lessons and two CVC-ending lessons, retry:

1. **Middle Sound — Basic**
2. **Ending Sound — Basic**
3. **First & Last Sound — Basic**
4. **Isolate All Sounds — Main**

`Isolate All Sounds` is the best title-level match to segmenting a complete ordered phoneme sequence, but the archive shows no Basic variant. That is why it belongs after the narrower printed-CVC bridges rather than now.

Then use:

5. **1-Syllable Words — Basic**

This is the closest archived next step toward applying phonics beyond a single position. The title and report taxonomy do not reveal whether its words are fully decodable from Student A's known correspondences, so its first attempt should be treated as a diagnostic.

### Phase 4: consonant blends, later

Begin consonant blends only after three-phoneme CVC blending and all-sound isolation are stable. The smallest archived entries are:

1. **Blends: st — Main**
2. **Blends: sp — Main**
3. **st, sp, sk, sm — Main**

These introduce four-phoneme words and adjacent consonants whose individual sounds must both be retained. They are a larger working-memory and ordering demand, and the first-grade entries do not have Basic variants. Digraphs, silent-e patterns, vowel teams, and multisyllable decoding should come later still.

## Mastery and zone-of-proximal-development rules

Continue using the transparent local policy in [`mastery-learning-policy.md`](mastery-learning-policy.md):

| Result | Decision |
|---:|---|
| 100% once | Mastered at that lesson variant; replace it with the next rung. |
| 90–99% once | Provisional; repeat the same rung. |
| Two consecutive scores ≥90% | Mastered at that rung; replace it with the next rung. |
| 80–89% | Stay on the rung; one repeat or a tightly matched prerequisite. |
| 50–79% | Step down to a narrower prerequisite; do not promote. |
| Below 50% | Step back further and reduce task complexity. |

These percentages are an operating rule, not a universal scientific boundary. The science-backed part is the feedback → corrective instruction → reassessment cycle and the requirement for cumulative success before adding complexity.

For this plan, “in the ZPD” means the next task changes only one major dimension:

- oral three-phoneme blending → printed CVC position;
- one short vowel → that vowel inside CVC words;
- one word position → another position;
- CVC → a four-phoneme blend only after CVC is stable.

Operationally, a lesson is probably well placed when Student A scores about 80–99% with improving or stable performance. A 100% score means it should be removed and replaced. Repeated scores below 80% mean the task is currently outside the desired instructional band or the activity itself is a poor fit.

## How *Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons* should influence this plan

The book's useful architecture is consistent with strong reading research: explicit directions, small cumulative steps, frequent response, correction, deliberate blending, and movement into word and text reading. Its “say it slowly, then say it fast” style also resembles connected phonation, which has direct empirical support.

However, the **exact book is not a research gold standard**. In its 2007 intervention review, the What Works Clearinghouse found no studies of the program that both fell within its beginning-reading protocol and met its evidence standards, so it could draw no effectiveness conclusion. ([WWC intervention review](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Intervention/512)) A later peer-reviewed parent-implemented multiple-probe study reported increased correct word reading and high parent fidelity, but it involved kindergarten children at risk for reading difficulty, 15 letter sounds, and 30 formatted lessons; it is promising small-design evidence, not a large randomized validation of the entire 100-lesson program. ([McConnell & Kubina, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1353/etc.2016.0010))

The right conclusion is therefore:

- Borrow the program's **instructional design principles**.
- Do not assume its exact sequence is uniquely optimal.
- Ground the skill order in the stronger phonemic-awareness and systematic-phonics evidence.
- Map that order conservatively to Khan lessons whose actual contents we can only infer from titles and report taxonomies.

## Confidence and limitations

**High confidence:** Student A should transition from isolated correspondences to ordered, print-linked CVC blending and segmentation; consonant blends and multisyllable words should wait.

**Moderate confidence:** `Blend Sounds 2 — Basic`, `Short Vowel Sound a — Basic`, `Words with a — Main`, and `Words with m & n — Main` are the lowest-gap Khan matches. Their titles and Khan taxonomy align well, but we have not independently coded every prompt inside each activity.

**Lower confidence:** Khan's labels `Basic`, `Main`, `Practice 1`, and `Practice 2` represent a strict psychometric difficulty ladder. Our family policy imposes that practical order, but the archive itself does not document the internal difficulty calibration.

The recommendations should be updated after every small batch of attempts. In particular, the first scores from short vowels and printed CVC-middle/ending groups will tell us whether Student A's low first-grade middle/end scores reflect a phonemic-position gap, an app-format gap, attention, or some combination.
